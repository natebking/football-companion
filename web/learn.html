American football for beginners: the rules, explained while you watch

American football looks complicated on TV, but most of it comes down to four ideas: downs, scoring, who is on the field, and penalties. This page covers each one in a few short paragraphs. The rules here apply to the NFL and to college football, with the main differences noted.

The basics: what is happening on the field?

Two teams of 11 players take turns with the ball. The field is 100 yards long, with a 10-yard end zone at each end. The team with the ball, the offense, tries to move it into the other team’s end zone. The defense tries to stop them.

The game is played in short bursts called plays. Each play starts with the snap, when the center hands or tosses the ball back between his legs, and ends when the ball carrier is tackled or goes out of bounds, a forward pass falls incomplete, or someone scores. The offense either runs with the ball or throws a forward pass. Only one forward pass is allowed per play, and it must be thrown from behind the line where the play started, the line of scrimmage.

A game has four 15-minute quarters. The clock stops often, so a game on TV usually lasts about three hours.

Downs: what does “3rd and 4” mean?

The offense gets four tries, called downs, to move the ball 10 yards. If it gets there, it earns a first down and four new tries. “3rd and 4” means it is the third try and the offense still needs 4 yards. On most broadcasts, the yellow line drawn across the field is the line the offense needs to reach.

If the offense has not made 10 yards after three tries, it usually uses fourth down to kick. It punts the ball deep to the other team, or tries a field goal if it is close enough. If it goes for it on fourth down and falls short, the other team takes over right there. That is called a turnover on downs.

The offense can also lose the ball by mistake. An interception is a pass caught by a defender. A fumble is a ball carrier losing the ball, and either team can recover it.

Scoring: how many points is a touchdown?

  • Touchdown, 6 points. Carry the ball into the end zone, or catch a pass there.
  • Extra point, 1 point. After a touchdown, kick the ball through the goalposts from short range.
  • Two-point conversion, 2 points. Instead of kicking, run or pass the ball into the end zone again from close in.
  • Field goal, 3 points. Kick the ball through the goalposts on any down, usually fourth.
  • Safety, 2 points for the defense. Tackle the ball carrier in his own end zone. The team that gave up the safety then kicks the ball away.

Positions: who does what?

Offense. The quarterback takes the snap and runs the play, usually by handing the ball off or throwing it. The running back takes handoffs and runs. Wide receivers line up near the sidelines to catch passes. The tight end lines up beside the line and both blocks and catches. Five offensive linemen, the center, two guards and two tackles, block to protect the quarterback and open running lanes.

Defense. Defensive linemen line up across from the offensive line and try to get to the ball carrier or the quarterback. Linebackers stand a few yards behind them, stop runs and cover short passes. Defensive backs, the cornerbacks and safeties, cover receivers and are the last line of defense.

Special teams. The kicker handles kickoffs, field goals and extra points. The punter punts on fourth down. Returners catch kicks and run them back.

Penalties: what does the yellow flag mean?

An official throws a yellow flag when a rule is broken. The usual punishment is moving the ball back or forward a set number of yards, and some fouls also give the offense an automatic first down. The team that was fouled can decline a penalty if the result of the play was better for it.

  • False start, 5 yards. An offensive player moves before the snap.
  • Offside, 5 yards. A defender is across the line of scrimmage when the ball is snapped.
  • Holding, 10 yards on the offense. A blocker grabs a defender to stop him. Holding by the defense gives the offense an automatic first down.
  • Pass interference. A player illegally blocks or grabs an opponent while the pass is in the air. Against the defense, it gives the offense the ball and a first down.
  • Personal foul, 15 yards. Dangerous or unsportsmanlike contact, such as a late hit.

How is college football different from the NFL?

The basics are the same. A few rules differ: a college receiver needs one foot in bounds for a catch, while the NFL requires two. Defensive pass interference in the NFL places the ball where the foul happened, while in college it is at most 15 yards. Defensive holding is 5 yards in the NFL and 10 in college, both with an automatic first down. Overtime also works differently in each. Fluent in Football follows both college (FBS) and NFL games.
